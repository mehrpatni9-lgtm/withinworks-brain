Reels

Reel strategy

How Megha shows up on video, and ten scripts ready to film. Tap a script to open it.

The idea

Posts show how Megha thinks. Reels show who she is. A stranger should hear her voice, see her calm, and leave thinking: she would be good to talk to. That feeling is what books a discovery call.

Why this suits her

  • She said yes to “a combination of video and personal blogs with some coaching facts”.
  • Coaching is a felt experience. A calm voice and a good question do more on video than any graphic.
  • Most coaches on Instagram talk at the camera. She asks it a question, and leaves space.

Four series

1 to 2 minutes · educational

Within, in a minute

One coaching idea, said plainly. What coaching is, what a coach listens for, how change actually happens.

1 to 2 minutes · storytelling

From the other chair

Her 25 years told honestly: what leading taught her, and what coaching asked her to unlearn.

15 to 20 seconds · thought provoking

The Within Question

The weekly question, spoken, with three seconds of real silence. Nobody else on the feed will leave a pause.

20 to 30 seconds · business

Coaching, plainly

Quick, warm answers to what people want to know before they book: what it is, how it works, what a first call is like.

How she films

  1. Phone on a small stand at eye level. No studio, no ring light.
  2. Window light, in her study or living room. Same two spots, so the feed feels like her home.
  3. No script on screen. Three bullet points on a card, then she talks.
  4. One take. A small pause or a restart stays in; it reads as real.
  5. Four reels in one morning, once a month. We edit: cuts, captions, cover.

Rhythm

  • One reel a week, long and short in turn, next to two posts.
  • Month one: S3 to introduce her, then L1, S1, L2.
  • Month two: L3, S2, L4, S4. Month three: L5, S5 and what worked.
  • Measured, never hustle. Quality over frequency.

What we watch

  • Saves and shares: it taught something.
  • Comments and DMs: it was felt.
  • Profile visits and discovery calls: they want more. This is the one that counts.
  • Not views. Views tell us who scrolled past.

Hook, first two seconds“For 25 years, my job was to have the answer.”

What she says

For 25 years, my job was to have the answer. Fourteen years in banking, at Citibank and Barclays. Then thirteen years at Traveazy, where we grew from ten people to more than three hundred, through a pandemic and two mergers.

People came to me with problems, and I was good at solving them. That was the job.

But the conversations I remember from those years aren’t the ones where I solved something. They’re the quieter ones. A capable person, doing well by every measure, saying almost in passing: “I’m not sure this is still me.”

I knew how to fix a process. I didn’t know how to sit with a question like that, and help someone find their own way through it.

L2 · Coach, mentor, consultant, therapistEducational · About 1:20 · Within, in a minute
Hook, first two seconds“People ask me how a coach is different from a mentor. Here’s how I see it.”
On screen: Coach? Mentor? Consultant? Therapist?

What she says

All four are valuable. They just do different jobs.

A therapist helps you heal what’s making everyday life feel unsteady. Often that means looking back. A mentor has walked the path you’re on, and shows you how they did it. A consultant studies the problem and hands you the plan.

A coach does something different. Coaching starts from the belief that you are already creative, resourceful and whole. Nothing needs fixing. My job isn’t to give you my answer. It’s to ask the questions that help you hear your own, and then walk beside you while you act on it.

So when does coaching make sense? Usually when you’re functioning well, but something feels unfinished. A decision you keep postponing. A role you’ve outgrown. A version of yourself you haven’t met yet.

And if what you need is a therapist or a mentor, a good coach will tell you so. I will.

Which one are you looking for right now?

Shot

Standing or seated, one take. Four words appear on screen as she names each role.

On-screen text

L3 · The word I listen forInsightful, intriguing · About 1:15 · What a coach hears
Hook, first two seconds“There’s one word I listen for in almost every conversation.”
On screen: The word I listen for

What she says

There’s one word I listen for in almost every conversation. Should.

“I should be further along by now.” “I should want this promotion.” “I should be able to handle it.”

When I hear it, I get curious. Not about the task. About the voice. Whose should is it?

Very often it isn’t yours. It belongs to a parent. A first boss. The culture you grew up in. A version of you from ten years ago who wanted different things. None of those voices are wrong. They just might not be current.

In coaching, we don’t argue with the should. We look at it. Where did it come from? Is it still true? What would you choose if it weren’t there? Once you can hear whose voice it is, you get to decide if you still agree.

This week, notice every time you say it. You don’t have to change anything. Just notice whose voice it sounds like.

Shot

Close and still. On “Should” the word appears large on screen and holds for a beat.

On-screen text

Should. · Whose should is it?

Caption

Some words tell me more than the sentence they’re in.

L4 · Ten people to three hundredStorytelling, insightful · About 1:30 · From the other chair
Hook, first two seconds“When we were ten people, I knew everyone’s story.”
On screen: 10 people. Then 300.

What she says

When Traveazy was ten people, I knew everyone’s story. By the time we were more than three hundred, through a pandemic and two mergers, I knew far less of it, and it mattered more.

Change at that scale teaches you something. The leaders who navigate it well are rarely the ones with all the answers. They’re the ones willing to be honest about not having them yet.

[Megha: one real moment from those years, no names. A hard week, a team meeting, a conversation that stayed with you.]

I didn’t have the language for it then. As a coach, I’d call it presence. Being fully in the room with what’s actually happening, not what you planned would happen.

It’s the thing I see leaders struggle with most, and the thing they’re most relieved to put down: the idea that they have to know.

So here’s my question for you. Where in your work right now are you pretending to know?

L5 · Same questions, different agesEmotional storytelling · About 1:30 · Turning points
Hook, first two seconds“My daughters are 21 and 16. Lately, we’re asking the same questions.”
On screen: Same questions, different ages

What she says

My daughters are 21 and 16. And lately I’ve noticed we’re asking the same questions.

What do I actually want? Who am I when I’m not being useful to someone? Is this path still mine, or did I just keep walking?

They’re asking at the start. I’m asking after 25 years of leading people and two careers. [Megha: add one personal turn only if you want to share it.]

I used to think those questions meant something was wrong. That a capable woman should have it figured out by now.

Coaching changed how I see them. A turning point isn’t a crisis. It’s information. Some part of you has outgrown the shape your life is in.

Many of the women I work with are in exactly this place. Doing well. Respected. And quietly asking, now what? I don’t hand them an answer. I help them find their own.

If you’re asking these questions too, at 25 or at 55, you’re not behind.
